Evaluation of micro-dwarf tomato cultivars  
for controlled environment research 
 
Noah J. Langenfeld and Bruce Bugbee 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
      Utah State University, Logan, UT 
 
 
Abstract 
 
Controlled environment research on tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) is often hindered by large plant 
size. Desired cultivar traits for research include a compact plant size, fast flowering time, and good fruit 
set. We grew eight micro-dwarf tomato cultivars to examine their suitability as model plants for research. 
The cultivar ‘Rejina Red’ had the highest harvest index and most ripe fruits among the red-fruited 
cultivars; ‘Jochalos’ had the highest harvest index and most ripe fruits among the yellow-fruited cultivars. 
‘Microtina’ had a higher harvest index than ‘Rosy Finch’, but fruits were smaller, and growth was less 
determinate. ‘Monetka’ had a germination rate of only 6% and low fruit set. ‘Rejina Red’ and ‘Jochalos’ 
had compact growth and excellent fruit set, and we recommend them for use in future tomato-based 
studies. 
 
Introduction 
Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) plants can 
be classified as having either determinate or 
indeterminate growth. Indeterminate cultivars 
continue growing throughout their life cycle and 
are often used for fresh-market fruit production. 
Determinate cultivars cease producing new 
shoots and buds beyond a smaller size (usually 
less than 1 m) and are typically grown for 
processing fruits (Maciel et al., 2016). Plants can 
further be classified by size as either traditional, 
dwarf, or micro-dwarf. While traditional plants 
can reach 10 m in length, dwarf cultivars rarely 
exceed 1 m, even for those with indeterminate 
growth behavior. Micro-dwarf cultivars are 
typcially less than 30 to 40 cm in height and can 
be much smaller. Most dwarf cultivars have been 
bred to produce traditionally sized fruit on a 
smaller 
plant 
to 
reduce 
growth 
area 
requirements, but micro-dwarf plants usually 
produce small fruits.  
 
Breeding for dwarf tomato cultivars increased 
in the late 1800s with the release of ‘Dwarf 
Champion’ (Livingston Seed Company, 1896). 
‘New Dwarf Champion’ was released in 1915 as 
an improved cultivar and is still grown today. 
Development of micro-dwarf cultivars was 
started in the 1980s by John Scott and Brent 
Harbaugh at the University of Florida Agricultural 
Experiment Station. They released ‘Micro-Tom’ 
in 1989 as one of the smallest tomato cultivars 
to date (Scott and Harbaugh, 1989) and it has 
since been used as a model cultivar for studying 
tomato genetics (Meissner et al., 1997). 
Crossing current cultivars with ‘Micro-Tom’ 
enabled overall plant dwarfing while maintaining 
fruit characteristics for both research and 
commercial purposes. For example, Kato et al. 
(2010) crossed ‘Micro-Tom’ with ‘Moneymaker’ 
to reduce plant size and make the study of 
protein 
production 
easier 
in 
controlled 
environments.  
Following the introduction of ‘Micro-Tom’, 
popularity was established and breeding of micro 
dwarf cultivars increased. ‘Micro-Gold’ (Scott 
and Harbaugh, 1995) was developed a few 
years later as a similar sized plant with yellow 
instead of red-colored fruits. Ten years later, 
‘Micro-Tina’ and ‘Micro-Gemma’ were developed 
as 
sweeter 
alternatives 
to 
their 
earlier 
counterparts (Scott et al., 2000). Throughout the 
early 21st century, interest in micro dwarf 
cultivars from home gardeners (Cruz et al., 
2022) and researchers grew and many cultivars

were released. The unique growth patterns, fruit 
sizes, fruit colors made choosing a suitable 
cultivar for research difficult.  
 
In this study, we grew eight micro-dwarf 
tomato cultivars and analyzed their germination 
rates, growth patterns, yields, and suitabilities as 
potential model crops. 
 
Methods 
The following cultivars were selected for their 
ranges of fruit color, fruit size, and growth 
patterns: ‘Venus’, ‘Microgemma’, ‘Jochalos’, 
‘Rosy Finch’, ‘Microtina’, ‘Rejina Red’, ‘Monetka’, 
and ‘Inkspot’.  
Three seeds of each cultivar were directly 
sown in 1.7-L containers with a soilless media 
containing 75% sphagnum peat moss, 13% 
vermiculite, and 12% parboiled rice hulls by 
volume. Hydrated lime was added at a rate of 1.5 
g per liter of media to increase the pH to 6.0 and 
wetting agent (AquaGro® 2000 G; Aquatrols, 
Paulsboro, NJ, USA) was added at a rate of 0.75 
g per liter of media to increase wetting ability of 
the hydrophobic peat.  
Plants were watered throughout their life 
cycle with a complete nutrient solution containing 
120 ppm N (72 ppm nitrate-N, 42 ppm 
ammonium-N, and 6 ppm urea-N), 31 ppm P, 
177 ppm K, 45 ppm Ca, 19 ppm Mg, 25 ppm S, 
17 ppm Si, 0.9 ppm Fe, 0.3 ppm Mn, 0.4 ppm Zn, 
0.41 ppm B, 0.85 ppm Cu, and 0.06 ppm Mo.  
Plants were grown in a glass greenhouse in 
Logan, UT, USA from October 2022 to January 
2023. The CO2 concentration was ambient at 
about 450 ppm, the relative humidity was about 
40%, and the photoperiod was 16 h with a DLI of 
about 25 mol m-2 s-1. 
 
All fruits were harvested 116 days after plants 
were seeded. Green and ripe (red or yellow 
depending on cultivar) fruits were separated and 
weighed along with shoots and leaves to 
determine the harvest index of each cultivar. 
 
Results 
‘Rejina Red’ and ‘Rosy Finch’ had the highest 
germination rate (94%) and Monetka had the 
lowest germination rate (6%) (Fig. 1). ‘Microtina’ 
was the first cultivar to flower (34 days) while 
‘Monetka’ and ‘Inkspot’ were the last to flower 
(47 days).

A visual comparison of cultivars is shown in 
Fig. 2. Among the yellow-fruited cultivars, 
‘Venus’ had the most compact growth and 
‘Jochalos’ had the least compact growth. Among 
the red-fruited cultivars, ‘Rejina Red’ had the 
most compact growth and ‘Microtina’ had the 
least compact growth (Fig. 2A). ‘Inkspot’ initially 
exhibited compact growth but trended toward 
bushy indeterminate growth later in its life cycle 
and ‘Monetka’ was not a healthy-looking plant 
and had poor fruit set (Fig. 2B).

Discussion 
Selection of desirable growth traits becomes 
more difficult as crops are pushed to smaller 
sizes. Limiting plant size while maintaining fruit 
set and fruit size has led to countless micro dwarf 
tomato cultivars developed over the past few 
decades. While ‘Microtina’ and ‘Microgemma’ 
have recently been used as model cultivars for 
dwarf tomato research, we wanted to explore 
additional 
cultivars 
with 
potentially 
more 
desirable characteristics.  
Cruz et al. (2022) previously studied twenty 
micro dwarf tomato cultivars for their suitability to 
container gardening for consumer horticulture in 
comparison to indoor greenhouse conditions. 
They compared the growth of plants grown in a 
growth chamber with a DLI of 11 mol m-2 s-1 with 
those grown into a greenhouse under natural 
sunlight conditions (8 to 18 mol m-2 s-1). They 
found a large variation in growth and yield 
among cultivars and observed intumescence on 
the smaller cultivars when grown under the low 
light conditions, which they hypothesized was 
from the lack of ultraviolet radiation. Of the 
cultivars they tested, only ‘Rosy Finch’ was the 
same as in our study. ‘Rosy Finch’ produced a 
similar number of fruits to the other cultivars they 
tested but had a higher yield than fifteen of the 
cultivars due to the larger fruit mass. We wanted 
to expand these results to screen for future 
potential 
cultivars 
for 
commercial 
and 
greenhouse systems where light would not be 
limiting. 
 
Germination percentage is usually a sign of 
the early vigor of a plant and an overall healthy 
cultivar (McDonald, 1980). Argerich et al. (1990) 
found that a slower germination and emergence 
was correlated with an increase in the 
percentage of green tomato fruits at harvest. 
This is similar to what we saw as both ‘Monetka’ 
and ‘Inkspot’ had the lowest germination 
percentages and subsequently took longest to 
flower and had some of the highest percentages 
of green fruits when harvested. Conversely, 
‘Rejina Red’ and ‘Rosy Finch’ had the highest 
germination rates and the most ripe fruits at 
harvest. Germination percentage can therefore 
be used as a reliable indicator of well-performing 
cultivars.

An important consideration for cultivar 
selection for research is the qualitative growth 
pattern of the plant. When fruits are hidden 
beneath leaves it becomes difficult for frequent 
and rapid assessment of fruit development. 
However, when fruits are exposed above the 
vegetative 
growth, 
monitoring 
of 
growth 
becomes easier. Both ‘Rejina Red’ and ‘Rosy 
Finch’ grew well, but the fruits of ‘Rejina Red’ 
were more exposed than the fruits of ‘Rosy 
Finch’. We found both visual monitoring and 
harvesting easier for ‘Rejina Red’ than ‘Rosy 
Finch’ due to this difference in growth pattern. 
 
While fruit size is a lower priority on a 
research level, it is often a high priority on a 
commercial level. As tomato fruit size increases, 
sugar content typically decreases (Casals et al., 
2018). Georgelis and Scott (2004) found five 
genetic markers associated with increased 
sugar contents were linked to reduced fruit size 
in tomato. While we did not conduct any 
consumer preference tests during this study, we 
did see a difference in fruit size among cultivars. 
The smaller fruit sizes of ‘Microtina’ and 
‘Microgemma’ tended to have thicker cuticles 
than the larger fruit sized cultivars of ‘Rejina Red’ 
and ‘Rosy Finch’. Thicker cuticles may be 
desirable in commercial production to reduce 
susceptibility to desiccation and improve shelf 
life (Fernández-Muñoz et al., 2022). 
 
Conclusion 
 
 ‘Rejina Red’ was the most promising, red-
fruited cultivar, and Jochalos was the most 
promising yellow fruited cultivar.     Both cultivars 
had high germination percentages, high fruit 
sets, and open, yet compact canopies for easy 
fruit monitoring throughout the lifecycle. These 
two cultivars are promising candidates for 
research studies in locations with limited space.
